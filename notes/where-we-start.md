# Where we start, and the order of the five problems

## The broken promise

"The problem" poses five problems in this order: the world, the rider's view, motion, the screen, the person watching. "A small story, a rich picture" answers them in that order. Then it says "We start there, with one triangle". That's the fourth problem. A reader who has just been handed a numbered list expects the book to walk it. We broke that promise in the last paragraph.

We have three honest ways out, and one dishonest one.

## The dishonest way

We could reorder the problem chapter to match the book we already have: put the screen first because Shapes comes first. That's reverse-engineering the problem from the solution. A reader can feel it, because the order has no reason inside the problem itself. It just happens to suit the author.

## Way 1: keep the order, and say why we jump

Keep the five problems as they stand. Then, at the end of the solution chapter, say plainly: we start with the fourth problem, because every other answer needs a way to show itself.

- **For it:** it's true, and it's cheap.
- **Against it:** it's a framed exception on page three. The book's first real move would be to break its own list. The second half breaks it again too, because motion (problem three) comes after the screen (problem four). One framed exception is fine. A list we never follow is a promise in name only.

## Way 2: walk the list as it stands

Write the book in the problem order: the world first, then the view, then motion, then the screen.

- **Against it:** the reader sees nothing until the fourth part. The world chapter has no pictures, since a picture is a list of shapes, and shapes come in part four. Motion would also land before the first still frame, which is the mistake you caught with "The clock". This fails your rule from this morning: master the basics, a tree and a cow, before placing things on the road.

## Way 3: find the order the problem really has

Ask what each problem needs before it can be solved. That's a property of the problem itself, not of our draft:

1. **Things.** Before you can show a world, you have to be able to show one thing in it: a tree, a cow, a tower. Each has to become shapes on a screen.
2. **The world.** Then something has to say where every one of those things stands, along a long road.
3. **The rider's view.** Then, for every frame, where each thing lands on the screen and how big, if the rider sees it at all, with nearer things covering farther ones.
4. **Motion.** Then the world has to move, step by step, and the moves have to fit together.
5. **The person watching.** Then the viewer needs controls they can find, answered straight away.

Each problem needs the one before it, and none needs the one after. That's the test that keeps it honest. You can't place a tree you can't draw. You can't see a road from a seat that doesn't exist. You can't move a frame you can't hold still. You can't rewind a ride that doesn't move. The order says how the problem is built, and the book is free to follow it.

It also matches the two halves the Introduction promises. One frame held still covers problems 1 to 3, and the story moving covers 4 and 5.

**What changes:**

- **The problem chapter's first problem stops being "the screen" and becomes "things".** Today's fourth problem ("reach the screen whole, nearer covering farther") splits in two. "Whole, from nothing" goes with things: a frame is a list of shapes, painted fresh. "Nearer covers farther" goes with the rider's view, where depth lives.
- **The opening sentence follows the new order:** "Safari Ride draws the things of a world, a whole road of them, as the rider sees them from the seat, in motion, and anyone watching can take the controls." That's a working line, not the final one.
- **The solution chapter answers in the same order:** shapes and pictures, then `World`, then the camera and `Pipeline`, then `RideInstant`, then `SafariApp` and `Tour`. The platform paragraph (the Roc code describes, a platform paints) moves to the first answer, where it belongs. It's the first thing a reader needs to know about putting a thing on the screen.
- **The book then walks the list:**
  - Shapes, then the simple tree, then the cow on the grass by the road: problem 1.
  - The world: problem 2.
  - Sightings, which is today's "The modules" material: problem 3.
  - The ride instant onward: problems 4 and 5.

  "The modules" stops being a map dropped after Shapes. Its sighting material becomes the start of problem 3.

## What I recommend

**Way 3.** The order comes from the problem, so it isn't cheating, and it's the order you already asked for: basics first. The cost is a rewrite of both short chapters, which are each a page, and a decision about the cow picture's place.

**The decision for you:** do we reorder the five problems by what each one needs (way 3), or keep the order and frame the jump (way 1)?
