# Two books in one day

Today the Roc book shipped its first edition. A Shale book went from five candidate paragraphs to a rough eight-chapter draft, then to a different book entirely, and that book's subject now runs on this machine. You've been replying to these essays on it. This essay looks back at how that happened, and why the second half went as well as it did.

## Finishing is its own skill

The Roc book's last day was small work with large effect. The early-draft notice came off. The appendices, which the published build had been quietly cutting, came back. The index's chapter numbers became links. None of it was writing. All of it was the difference between a draft and an edition.

Each fix also left the book's machinery a little more honest. The cut that dropped the appendices now counts only numbered chapters. The index text, which no check had ever read, is now checked like the rest of the prose. And that check found its own first bug on the way in: rows of links were being cut off at 200 bytes. Finishing a book this way means the gates get better every time the book does.

## A draft made to be thrown away

The first Shale draft was one-shot on purpose, and that's the part worth remembering. The Roc book's retrospective named one-shotting as its costliest mistake: we drafted fast and paid for a spine we never chose. This time the one-shot came first, with no plan to keep it. It cost an hour, and it was a probe.

The probe worked because it gave you something to react to. Your reactions were the real output:
- "It reads like a collection of Steve's useful scripts."
- Programmers want to write apps, not books.
- You never read the header comments.
- Gates should come early and stay.

None of those would have come out of a planning conversation. A draft, even a rough one, asks better questions than an outline does, as long as everyone agrees it's a draft.

So the Roc book's first lesson, *define the spine before drafting*, turned out to need a qualifier. Define the spine before drafting the book you'll keep. A disposable draft is one of the best tools for finding the spine.

## The pivot

The new concept took the Roc book's best lessons and made them literal.

**Choose a codebase small enough to quote.** The Roc book quoted a large program and struggled to show its hard parts. The Shale book builds its codebase from nothing, in a week of chapters, so every line has a reason to be there and a day it arrived.

**Keep the language's thread strong in every chapter.** The Roc book's second half drifted toward the program and away from the language. Here, every chapter is Shale doing real work: the agent's own scripts, read the way you read them as they scroll by.

**Teach the unique things at full strength.** Gates, plants and releases aren't a late chapter. They arrived on their own: a check saved unprompted on Monday, a planted fault on Wednesday, a release script on Friday. Your survey of the old essay surface showed it had never needed much gating, so nothing here had to be contrived. The need grew with the project, which is the honest way to teach it.

**Write from real work.** This one is new. The Roc book was written about a program. This one is written from five journals, kept by five agents, of work that really happened. The prompts are ours, quoted whole. The mistakes are real ones: invisible reply buttons, an unescaped name, a test whose panic hid twelve others, my own garbled copy commands. Each one comes with what caught it, which is what makes a mistake instructive and not embarrassing.

## The tool became the medium

The moment I'll remember is your first reply on the new surface. It was to a bullet about a rename pass, and it read back correctly from a file I could diff. From then on, the project was also the place we talked about the project. You found the hidden buttons because you were using it. You asked for "reply" instead of "note" because the word mattered in use. The release script exists because I'd swapped your server by hand five times and you said that's what scripts are for.

Dogfooding did something the book needs: it turned the reader's question, "would I actually work this way?", into something we answered by doing it.

## Shale is meant to change

The week also gave the book its biggest selling point. A nuisance came up four times: a run that stopped left its server running. So an agent is changing Shale itself, tonight, so a stopped run stops what it started. Your framing makes this a feature, not a fork: Shale is meant to have dialects. Dialects cost humans a lot and agents very little, so each reader keeps the dialect that works for their agent, and builds their own recipes on top.

That reframes the whole premise. The reader learns a language they read and rarely write, and their agent shapes it to fit the work.

## What made the day work

- **One thread for you, many lanes for me.** Five agents at a time at the peak, and you only ever had one task.
- **Prompts written before dispatch, in your voice,** so the book can quote what we really asked.
- **Every decision on the essay surface,** and by the afternoon, on the new essay surface.
- **Small, real, reversible steps:** a local repo with no remote, a dev port, copies of your data, a release with a rollback.
