# The game tie-ins: what your answers taught me

## Did you find the plant?

No, and it's the more interesting result. The plant was **#4, the two riders racing in lockstep**. I built it to be true and well argued but aimed in the wrong direction: a multiplayer network race is a big leap for a book whose ride has one rider and no input yet. You called it "very strong", which means the craft held up. Then you named exactly why it was the plant: "jumping the gun past simpler ways to improve the one-player experience." You rejected the premise without flagging the paragraph. That's the plant doing its job: a polished paragraph with a wrong premise reads as strong, and only the premise gives it away.

You picked **#6, the family spotting giraffes**, and your reason was sharp: "You don't describe any real user agency here." That's a fair hit on a pitch I meant sincerely. Spotting and counting is watching with a scorecard, not playing.

## What surprised me

1. **Agency is the test.** Every pitch you liked hands the player something to *do*: react to the cat, take the throttle. Every pitch you cooled on gave them something to *watch* or *know*: a score, a lockstep, a type guarantee. I'd been pitching what the code makes possible. You were judging what the player gets to feel.

2. **"Experience over score."** I reached for a score twice, as the obvious mark of a game. You pointed out that first-person games are often about the experience. That changes the reflex pitch's shape: the fun is the near miss and the wipe-out, not the number at the end.

3. **You wanted the mature reader.** I expected you to prefer the pitches aimed at game enthusiasts. Instead the loop pitch (#3) won you over because it spoke to a working programmer, and the determinism pitch (#5) did too, once it ties to Roc. That matches the book's mission: its reader is a working programmer, not a gamer.

4. **Roc is the hook, not games.** Your one request on #5 was to tie determinism to Roc. The tie-in isn't "this could be a game". It's "Roc makes the hard parts of a game come naturally". The game is the occasion, and Roc is the point.

5. **#2 failed on the rule I wrote down.** "Nothing in it can be in two minds about anything" is a D2 violation, and agent-speak besides, and you stopped reading there. I broke our own rule in a pitch. A cold reader on these would have caught it; I skipped one because the pitches were short. That's a lesson for me: short isn't safe.

## The two new versions

The new note riffs on your feedback, blind again:
- one aims at the mature programmer, ties determinism to Roc, and drops "games rarely have it";
- one gives the player the handlebars, with braking, wipe-outs into critters, and feel over score.

You suggested the reflex angle belongs near the end of the introduction, and both new pitches are written to sit there.
